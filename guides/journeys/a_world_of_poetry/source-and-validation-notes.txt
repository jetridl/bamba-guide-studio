ORDER AND EDITORIAL ARC

1. The Arrow and the Song — Henry Wadsworth Longfellow. Storytelling: a complete miniature narrative, paired sounds and delayed discovery make an accessible opening.
2. A Red, Red Rose — Robert Burns. Love: the song motif becomes the music of a personal declaration, with Scots vocabulary gently introduced.
3. The Sick Rose — William Blake. Symbolism: a familiar flower becomes uncertain; eight lines make space for several interpretations.
4. A Dream Within a Dream — Edgar Allan Poe. Uncertainty: the difficulty of interpreting an image widens into questions of reality, time and loss, grounded in a hand holding sand.
5. Because I could not stop for Death — Emily Dickinson. Mortality: a familiar carriage ride gives Death a social presence; the correction at the fourth stanza complicates the apparent journey.
6. The Road Not Taken — Robert Frost. Choices: the difference between the similar roads and a future retelling invites irony and careful interpretation, without replacing one simplistic moral with another.
7. O Captain! My Captain! — Walt Whitman. Grief: a sustained ship-and-voyage metaphor joins public history with personal address; long and short lines invite expressive but unforced recitation.

Complexity grows chiefly in emotional and interpretive demands, rather than line count. Blake is the shortest selection and still deepens the reading. Whitman's long lines require more sustained breath at the close.
The introduction explicitly describes this as a small English-language selection from Scotland, England and the United States, not a survey of world poetry. The closing opens towards other languages, traditions and living voices.
None of the three First Steps in Poetry poems is repeated. Neither of the other repository Journey files duplicates any of these seven poems. No files for the other planned Journeys were available; their future selections cannot be checked for overlap.

VISUAL REVIEW

The cover reads as a traditional architectural oil painting. Its large arched windows, dark oak presses and clear floor plane remain recognisable at card size. Natural perspective and receding bays establish a coherent interior; varied brushwork describes stone, timber, glass and shadow without a photographic filter or uniform texture overlay.
Soft window light, warm neutral stone and subdued wood keep the atmosphere inviting. No extreme golden light, decorative glow, visible typography, signatures, watermark, figures or staged writing props appear. Shelved books belong to the library rather than functioning as added poetry props.
The exposed timber roof and distant college architecture are part of an imaginative Cambridge setting, not a documentary claim about the Wren Library. The final alt text describes what is visible in the generated painting.
