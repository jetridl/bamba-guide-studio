# If— — Study Guide

**Rudyard Kipling, 1910 · 8 Days · 32 Lines**

---

## Reference: The Full Poem

If you can keep your head when all about you   
    Are losing theirs and blaming it on you,   
If you can trust yourself when all men doubt you,
    But make allowance for their doubting too;   
If you can wait and not be tired by waiting,
    Or being lied about, don’t deal in lies,
Or being hated, don’t give way to hating,
    And yet don’t look too good, nor talk too wise:

If you can dream—and not make dreams your master;   
    If you can think—and not make thoughts your aim;   
If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster
    And treat those two impostors just the same;   
If you can bear to hear the truth you’ve spoken
    Twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools,
Or watch the things you gave your life to, broken,
    And stoop and build ’em up with worn-out tools:

If you can make one heap of all your winnings
    And risk it on one turn of pitch-and-toss,
And lose, and start again at your beginnings
    And never breathe a word about your loss;
If you can force your heart and nerve and sinew
    To serve your turn long after they are gone,   
And so hold on when there is nothing in you
    Except the Will which says to them: ‘Hold on!’

If you can talk with crowds and keep your virtue,   
    Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch,
If neither foes nor loving friends can hurt you,
    If all men count with you, but none too much;
If you can fill the unforgiving minute
    With sixty seconds’ worth of distance run,   
Yours is the Earth and everything that’s in it,   
    And—which is more—you’ll be a Man, my son!

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** When the room turns on you

**Body:**
The first four lines describe a specific predicament. You are at the centre of something going wrong. Others are panicking. Worse, they have decided the panic is your fault. Worse still, they have begun to doubt your judgement out loud.

Kipling does not flinch from how lonely this is. The room is against you. The poem's answer is not righteousness or counter-attack. It is composure, paired with a peculiar generosity in line 4: even as you trust yourself, you allow that the doubters might have their reasons. That qualification is what saves the lines from sounding smug.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** The doubter and the doubted

**Body:**
The first word of the poem is "If". So is the first word of line 3. The whole poem will be a piling up of conditions before any reward arrives. We will not learn what you get for all this until the second-to-last line. That is a long suspended sentence to hold.

Look at the symmetry of line 3 and line 4. "Trust yourself when all men doubt you" is the easy heroism. "But make allowance for their doubting too" is the harder discipline. Self-belief without humility curdles fast. Kipling closes the loop in a single half-line.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The poet

**Heading:** A boy from Bombay

**Body:**
Rudyard Kipling was born in Bombay in 1865. His parents, both English, had moved to India for work. He spent his early childhood there, surrounded by Indian languages and stories, before being sent at age five to a foster home in Southsea, England. The years there were miserable. He later called the place "the House of Desolation".

He returned to India as a young journalist in his teens and made his name there. By the time he wrote "If—", he was the most widely read writer in the English-speaking world. The British public adored him. Critics, then as now, were more divided.

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** Make allowance

**Body:**
The phrase "make allowance" is doing more work than it looks. It is not just tolerance. To make allowance for someone is to grant them the conditions of their misjudgement, to admit that from where they stand, the doubt is reasonable.

That generosity changes the speaker's position. He is not above the doubters; he is among them, having simply decided not to let their doubt unsteady him. The poem will return to this stance of poised humility throughout.

# DAY 2 — Patience without bitterness

*Lines 5–8. The first stanza closes with a harder demand: not just to stay calm, but to refuse the easy revenges of lying, hating, and looking pleased about it.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 2 of 8

**Heading:** What we keep doing

**Body:**
Yesterday we met the speaker in the middle of trouble: doubted, blamed, surrounded by panic. The answer was composure, paired with the quiet generosity of "make allowance for their doubting too."

Today the lens widens. The trouble lasts. The waiting drags on. People lie about you. Some of them hate you. The poem now asks how you behave when you have time to nurse a grudge.

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=5-8]*

> If you can wait and not be tired by waiting,
>     Or being lied about, don’t deal in lies,
> Or being hated, don’t give way to hating,
>     And yet don’t look too good, nor talk too wise:

This is the close of stanza one. The colon at the end of line 8 will not let us off; it pulls us straight on into stanza two tomorrow.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** Three refusals and a warning

**Body:**
The structure of these four lines is a list of refusals. Refuse to be tired by waiting. Refuse to lie back when lied about. Refuse to hate when hated. Then a sting in the tail: don't look too good, and don't talk too wise.

The last line is the one that keeps the stanza honest. Without it, the speaker would be reciting a Sunday-school lesson. With it, he reminds us that the moment you start to feel saintly about your patience, you have lost the thing entirely.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** Don't, don't, don't

**Body:**
Kipling repeats "don't" three times across lines 6 and 7. Each one is paired with a passive participle: "being lied about", "being hated". The grammar is doing the moral work. You are passive in the wrong; you must be active in not returning it.

Then comes "And yet". Two small words that turn the lines. Everything before "yet" is what to refuse. The phrase after "yet" warns you against being seen to refuse it. Virtue that advertises itself stops being virtue. This is the door into the stanzas that follow.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Form

**Heading:** One sentence per stanza

**Body:**
Each of the four stanzas in "If—" is a single sentence. Eight lines, no full stop until the colon at the end. The reader is held in suspension while conditions accumulate. Speak it aloud and you can feel the pressure building in the lungs.

The rhyme scheme of stanza one is unusual. The first four lines all close on "you" or "too", a wall of second-person address. Then the rhyme opens up. The speaker has been pinning the reader in place with that "you" and only now lets the air in.

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** The look that gives you away

**Body:**
"Don't look too good, nor talk too wise." This is the cleverest line in the stanza, and the one most often skipped over. The whole poem is about a man holding himself together. But the moment he is seen to be holding himself together, something is lost. Self-possession that performs itself becomes a kind of vanity.

Kipling, who would later be accused of every kind of moralising, plants the warning here, at the threshold. The reader is being told, before any reward is on the table, not to wear any of this on his face.

# DAY 3 — Dreams, thoughts, and the two impostors

*Lines 9–12. The poem opens its second stanza by domesticating dreams and thoughts, then puts Triumph and Disaster in their place.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 3 of 8

**Heading:** Inside the head

**Body:**
The first stanza was the world acting on you. Today's chunk is what you do with what is inside you.

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=9-12]*

> If you can dream—and not make dreams your master;   
>     If you can think—and not make thoughts your aim;   
> If you can meet with Triumph and Disaster
>     And treat those two impostors just the same;   

These four lines contain perhaps the most famous moral instruction in English verse. The two-line couplet about Triumph and Disaster is engraved at the players' entrance to Centre Court at Wimbledon, where it has been read by every tennis champion of the modern era on their way out to win or lose. We will spend time today with the word that does most of its work, which is not "Triumph" or "Disaster" but "impostors".

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** Servants, not masters

**Body:**
Notice what Kipling does to dreams in line 9. He does not say don't dream. He says don't let dreaming become the thing that runs you. The same with thinking in line 10. Both are tools, useful in their place, dangerous when they take the wheel.

The pair of dashes in lines 9 and 10 is doing pointed work. The break sets up a small "yes, but" rhythm. Yes, dream. But. Yes, think. But. The reader is allowed the gift before it is qualified.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** Two impostors

**Body:**
"Impostors" is the word doing the heavy lifting. To call Triumph and Disaster impostors is to say neither one is what it appears. Triumph is not the proof you are good. Disaster is not the proof you are finished. Both will overstate themselves, and both should be greeted with the same level look.

The thought is older than Kipling. It is Stoic. It is in the Book of Job. It is in countless soldier's letters. The reason it survives in this form is that one word. "Impostors" does the work of three sentences in one breath.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The poet

**Heading:** A man who knew disaster

**Body:**
Kipling was not writing this from theory. In 1899 his eldest daughter Josephine died of pneumonia in New York. She was six. Kipling, who had been gravely ill himself in the same hotel, recovered to find she had not. He wrote almost nothing public about her loss for the rest of his life.

Triumph he had in plenty. The Nobel Prize for Literature came in 1907; he was the first English-language writer to receive it. By the time "If—" was published in 1910, he was famous enough that the poem became a phenomenon almost immediately, and he lived to watch it ossify into a national possession.

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** Master and aim

**Body:**
Lines 9 and 10 are a near-mirror. "Master" and "aim" land in the same metrical position. Both turn an inner activity into a danger when it grows too large. Dreams that master you become fantasy. Thoughts that become an aim become an end in themselves, where reflection replaces action.

These are warnings against the intellectual life as much as against fecklessness. Kipling, who could think and dream as productively as anyone alive, is telling the reader what those gifts are for. They serve the day. They are not the day.

# DAY 4 — Truth twisted, work broken

*Lines 13–16. The second stanza closes with two scenes of public ruin and a quiet act of repair.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 4 of 8

**Heading:** What survives the bad days

**Body:**
Yesterday Triumph and Disaster were the abstractions. Today they take particular shapes.

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=13-16]*

> If you can bear to hear the truth you’ve spoken
>     Twisted by knaves to make a trap for fools,
> Or watch the things you gave your life to, broken,
>     And stoop and build ’em up with worn-out tools:

These are the bleakest lines in the poem so far. The speaker has been honest, and his honesty has been weaponised. The speaker has built something, and it has been broken in front of him. The lines accept that this happens, and ask only what he will do next.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** Knaves and fools

**Body:**
A knave, in the older sense, is a dishonest schemer. A fool, here, is the credulous person who falls for the schemer's trick. Kipling sets them up as a pair. The knave twists what you said; the fools fall into the trap.

You are caught in the middle. You spoke the true thing. The true thing has been bent into a snare. The injury is double. You have been misrepresented, and someone else has been harmed by the misrepresentation. The line asks only that you bear it.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** Stoop and build

**Body:**
Listen to the rhythm of line 16. "And stoop and build 'em up with worn-out tools." The verb "stoop" comes first because it is the harder act. Bending to pick up the broken pieces is what costs you. The building comes after, and it gets done with what is left.

"Worn-out tools" is an honest detail. The poem does not pretend you will get fresh equipment for round two. You start again with what you already have, which is less than you began with, and you do it anyway. The contracted "'em" keeps the line in working clothes; nothing in it is heroic.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The poet

**Heading:** Kipling under fire

**Body:**

# DAY 5 — One throw, silent loss

*Lines 17–20. The poem's most reckless image: gathering everything, betting it on a single coin toss, losing, and saying nothing.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 5 of 8

**Heading:** Risk and silence

**Body:**
Yesterday we watched truth twisted and work broken. The poem's response was to stoop and rebuild. Today the poem dares more.

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=17-20]*

> If you can make one heap of all your winnings
>     And risk it on one turn of pitch-and-toss,
> And lose, and start again at your beginnings
>     And never breathe a word about your loss;

The image is brutal in its simplicity. Everything you have won, in one pile. One throw of a coin. Lose, start over, and do not complain.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** Pitch-and-toss

**Body:**
Pitch-and-toss is a children's gambling game played with coins. Players stand at a line and pitch their coins toward a target. Whoever lands closest wins; the rest is decided by tossing for heads or tails. It is the simplest possible bet, with no skill once the coin is in the air.

Kipling chooses the cheapest game on purpose. The man in the lines is not at a casino with mahogany tables. He is in a back yard, putting his life's winnings on a coin. The grandeur of what he risks is offset by the meanness of the throw.

# DAY 6 — The Will that holds on

*Lines 21–24. The third stanza closes with the poem's most exhausted and most defiant image: a body running on nothing but its own will.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 6 of 8

**Heading:** Past empty

**Body:**
Yesterday the loss was financial and silent. Today the demand goes deeper, into the body itself.

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=21-24]*

> If you can force your heart and nerve and sinew
>     To serve your turn long after they are gone,   
> And so hold on when there is nothing in you
>     Except the Will which says to them: ‘Hold on!’

These lines have been quoted in every kind of context, from war journalism to running club newsletters. They are about pushing past the point where pushing on should not be possible.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** Heart, nerve, sinew

**Body:**
Three nouns share line 21: heart, nerve, sinew. Together they cover the working parts of the body that fail under strain. The heart for courage and circulation. The nerve for the willingness to act. The sinew for the muscle that does the work.

All three are spoken of as servants in line 22. They serve "your turn", a phrase from card games and from soldiering, meaning the moment when it is on you to act. When your turn comes round, these are what you have. When they are gone, the question is whether anything remains.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** The capital W

**Body:**
"Will" arrives at line 24 with a capital. In a poem otherwise restrained about its abstractions, this stands out. Triumph and Disaster were also capitalised, and they were called impostors. The Will is the only one the poem treats as real.

Notice that Will is given speech. It says "Hold on!" to the body. The body has stopped having anything to say. The Will is the voice that goes on speaking when the speaker himself is silent. This is the closest the poem comes to mysticism, and it is still couched in the plainest language.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Tradition

**Heading:** Stoicism in plain English

**Body:**
The thinking behind these lines is older than the lines themselves. Marcus Aurelius wrote out the same instructions to himself in Greek in his tent on the Danube in the second century. The Stoics held that the only thing entirely yours is your response. The body fails, the world breaks, but the choice to keep going belongs to you.

Kipling never quotes the Stoics, but he had been schooled in the Latin classics at the United Services College in Devon and would have known the lineage. Part of the poem's power is that it strips that ancient idea of its philosophical robes and gives it to anyone who can read.

# DAY 7 — Crowds, Kings, common touch

*Lines 25–28. The final stanza opens with a balancing act: keeping yourself in any company, from the marketplace to the throne room.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 7 of 8

**Heading:** Among others again

**Body:**
After the body's exhaustion in stanza three, the poem returns to the social world for the last time.

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=25-28]*

> If you can talk with crowds and keep your virtue,   
>     Or walk with Kings—nor lose the common touch,
> If neither foes nor loving friends can hurt you,
>     If all men count with you, but none too much;

These four lines describe a particular kind of equilibrium. Move freely between the high and the low without belonging to either. Be open to other people without being shaped by them. It is harder than it sounds.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** Crowds and Kings

**Body:**
"Crowds" and "Kings" are the polar pair, with Kings capitalised to give them their full weight. The line is not really about literal kings. It is about the rooms where power lives. The challenge is to walk into one of those rooms and not become a different person, and to walk back into a market square and not become a different person there either.

Kipling moved in both worlds. He was friends with George V and with sergeants in barracks rooms. He thought the test was whether you used the same voice in both places.

**Body:**

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The poet

**Heading:** A man who refused honours

**Body:**
By the time he wrote "If—", Kipling had already declined offers of the Poet Laureateship and a knighthood. He would later turn down the Order of Merit as well. He accepted the Nobel Prize in 1907, but he was wary of any institutional embrace. He thought the official recognitions would soften him.

He kept the company he wanted to keep. He moved between dukes and dock workers without much fuss either way. The line "walk with Kings, nor lose the common touch" was an idea he tried to live by, with the irritating fluency of a man who actually had access to both.

**Body:**
"All men count with you, but none too much" sits at the centre of the poem's social ethic. Not indifference: every person matters. Not idolatry: nobody owns you. The line refuses two easy stances at once.

# DAY 8 — The unforgiving minute

*Lines 29–32. The conditions end, and the long-suspended sentence finally lands.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 8 of 8

**Heading:** The arrival

**Body:**
For seven days the poem has been listing conditions. Today the conditions stop and the reward arrives.

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=29-32]*

> If you can fill the unforgiving minute
>     With sixty seconds’ worth of distance run,   
> Yours is the Earth and everything that’s in it,   
>     And—which is more—you’ll be a Man, my son!

These are the lines the rest of the poem has been waiting for. Read them once aloud before we look at them. Notice how the poem has piled up its conditions for thirty lines, and only here does it tell you what you get for keeping all of them.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Today's chunk

**Heading:** The unforgiving minute

**Body:**
A minute has sixty seconds. Kipling tells you to fill it with sixty seconds' worth of distance run. The instruction is mathematical, almost punitive. There are no spare seconds. A minute that is not filled is a minute that is gone, and minutes do not come back.

"Unforgiving" is the word. The minute will not pardon you for slack use of it. The image comes from running, possibly from the athletics field at school, but it travels easily into any kind of work. The clock is a hard master.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close reading

**Heading:** Yours is the Earth

**Body:**
After all the conditions, the reward in line 31 is enormous. "Yours is the Earth and everything that's in it." A reader expecting something modest gets the planet. The phrasing has the cadence of the King James Bible, where similar absolute promises live.

But the poem does not stop there. Line 32 quietly raises the stakes a second time. "And—which is more—you'll be a Man, my son!" The Earth is the lesser prize. Becoming the kind of person who could meet all those conditions is the greater one. The closing word, "son", finally names the listener who has been spoken to all along.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The poet

**Heading:** A father, a son

**Body:**
Kipling had a son, John, born in 1897. The poem was published in 1910, when John was thirteen. It was natural that readers heard the closing line as addressed to him.

In 1915, John was killed at the Battle of Loos. He was eighteen. Kipling had used his connections to get him a commission despite poor eyesight that should have kept him out of the army. The boy died in his first action, and his body was not identified for many years. Kipling wrote little about it directly. Among the few public lines he gave to it was a couplet: "If any question why we died, / Tell them, because our fathers lied." The poem you are learning was already in print and already famous when its closing line acquired a second meaning that its author would have given anything to undo.
