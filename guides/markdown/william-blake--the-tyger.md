# The Tyger — Study Guide

**William Blake, 1794 · 6 Days · 24 Lines**

---

## Reference: The Full Poem

Tyger Tyger, burning bright,
In the forests of the night;
What immortal hand or eye,
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?

In what distant deeps or skies.
Burnt the fire of thine eyes?
On what wings dare he aspire?
What the hand, dare seize the fire?

And what shoulder, & what art,
Could twist the sinews of thy heart?
And when thy heart began to beat,
What dread hand? & what dread feet?

What the hammer? what the chain,
In what furnace was thy brain?
What the anvil? what dread grasp,
Dare its deadly terrors clasp!

When the stars threw down their spears
And water'd heaven with their tears:
Did he smile his work to see?
Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

Tyger Tyger burning bright,
In the forests of the night:
What immortal hand or eye,
Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The Poet

**Heading:** Blake the Visionary

**Body:**
William Blake was born in London in 1757, the son of a hosier on Broad Street. As a small boy he saw a tree filled with angels at Peckham Rye and ran home to tell his parents. His father considered beating him for the lie. His mother intervened. He went on seeing things for the rest of his life.

He trained as an engraver. He never went to a university, never travelled outside England, and never made much money. He worked at a press in his small house in Lambeth, hand-printing and hand-colouring his books one copy at a time. To his neighbours he was a strange man muttering to himself in the garden. To us, now, he is one of the most original minds the language has produced.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Burning Bright

**Body:**
Notice the meter. Trochaic tetrameter: stressed-unstressed, four times a line. *TY-ger TY-ger BURN-ing BRIGHT.* It is the rhythm of a chant or a drum, the kind of beat children fall into without trying. Blake wanted the poem to land in the body before it landed in the mind.

Then the strangeness: "fearful symmetry". Symmetry is order, balance, a thing made well. Fearful is what such order does to you when you face it. Blake puts the two words together and asks whether terrible beauty can be one thing rather than two. The whole poem is a test of that question.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The Book

**Heading:** Songs of Experience

**Body:**
"The Tyger" appeared in 1794 in *Songs of Experience*, the darker sequel to Blake's *Songs of Innocence* of five years before. He bound them together as one book with a long subtitle: *Shewing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul*. Innocence on one side, experience on the other. The Lamb on one page, the Tyger on another.

Each copy of the book was a small handmade object. Blake etched the pages onto copper plates that carried both his words and his illustrations. He printed them on a press in his rented rooms, and his wife Catherine helped colour them by hand. No two surviving copies are quite alike.

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** The Question Asked Sideways

**Body:**
The first line is not a sentence. It is an invocation, a name spoken twice as if to summon what it names. Then the second line plants the tiger in the dark, and the next two lines pivot into the question that the rest of the poem will keep asking in different shapes.

Look at the verb in line four: "Could frame". Not "did" frame. Blake is not asking whether the tiger was made. He is asking what kind of being would be capable of making such a thing at all. That distinction matters. It governs everything that follows.

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The Poet

**Heading:** Blake's London

**Body:**
Blake spent almost his entire life inside a few square miles of London. He walked Soho and Lambeth and the riverside until he knew them like a private mythology. The London of the 1790s was crowded, loud, lit by oil lamps, and politically electric. The American Revolution had ended a decade before. The French one had just begun.

Blake knew the radicals of the day. He drank with Thomas Paine. He read Mary Wollstonecraft. He briefly wore the red cap of the French revolutionaries through the streets of London until it became too dangerous and he stopped. The poem in front of you was made by a man who knew what fire could do in the world, not only in heaven.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Distant Deeps

**Body:**
"In what distant deeps or skies." The line ends with a full stop where you expect a question mark, and editors have argued about whether it is a printer's slip or a deliberate held breath. Either way the effect is to slow you down, to make you feel how far off these deeps are before the next line strikes the eyes back into fire.

The shape of the stanza is a triple chase. Three questions, each one daring more. The eyes' fire, the wings of the maker, the hand that grabs the flame. Behind it all you can hear Prometheus, who stole fire from the gods, and Icarus, who flew too near the sun. Blake never names them. He does not need to.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Imagery

**Heading:** Why Fire

**Body:**
In Blake's private mythology, fire is not punishment. It is energy itself, the thing without which there is no life. "Energy is Eternal Delight," he wrote in *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*, the book he was working on around the same time as *Songs of Experience*. The flame in the tiger's eyes is therefore not a sign of damnation. It is the same stuff the stars and the sun are made of, packed into a body that walks at night.

Which makes the question on this page sharper, not gentler. If the tiger burns with the same fire as the heavens, what kind of being would dare gather it up and pour it into a beast?

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** What the Hand Dared

**Body:**
The verb to watch is "dare". It enters the poem here, on the third question of the stanza, and it changes the temperature. To frame is to make. To dare is to know you should not, and to do it anyway. The maker is no longer a craftsman. The maker is a trespasser.

Notice too the pronoun: "he". Until now the poem has spoken of an "immortal hand or eye", abstract and sexless. Suddenly there is a he, a someone, a figure with wings who can reach. The poem has begun to move from a question about a thing to a question about a person.

# DAY 3 — Sinews of the Heart

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The Poet

**Heading:** Catherine and the Press

**Body:**
Blake was twenty-four when he married Catherine Boucher, the daughter of a market gardener. She could not read. He taught her. Then he taught her to print, and for the next forty-five years she stood at the press with him, inking the plates, pulling the impressions, colouring the pages by hand.

She was, by all accounts, devoted and unflustered. When Blake had visions she made him tea. When the household ran out of money, which was often, she went without dinner so he could keep working. The book you are studying came off her press too. There is a sense in which she made the tiger as much as he did.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Shoulder and Art

**Body:**
"And what shoulder, & what art." Blake places a worker's body part next to an abstract noun, and the line refuses to choose between them. To make this creature you would need both: physical labour and aesthetic skill, brute force and design. The ampersand is doing real work. It binds them in one breath.

Then "twist the sinews of thy heart". Sinews are tendons. Twisting them is anatomy as craft. The maker is not waving a wand. The maker is winding cords inside a body. By the end of line ten the tiger is already half-built, and you have not been spared a single step.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The Plate

**Heading:** Blake's Drawing

**Body:**
On the original etched plate of "The Tyger", Blake placed an illustration of the animal beneath the text. Generations of readers have arrived at it expecting menace, and found instead a slightly worried-looking cat. The tiger in the picture is gentle. Some critics find it almost comic.

Many readers take this as a deliberate gap. The poem reaches for sublime terror; the illustration shows a creature you might pat. Whatever Blake meant by the mismatch, it warns us that the poem's tiger is not a zoological observation. It is something the imagination assembles in the dark.

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Dread

**Body:**
Lines eleven and twelve do something the poem has not done before. They jump in time. "And when thy heart began to beat" is the moment of life. The maker has just lit the body. And immediately the next line breaks into fragments: "What dread hand? & what dread feet?"

Dread, twice in a single line. The word means more than fear. It means a fear that comes with awe, the kind a small thing feels when it stands in front of a vast one. The closer the poem gets to the moment the tiger comes alive, the harder the speaker has to work to keep speaking. The grammar is starting to shake.

# DAY 4 — Hammer, Chain, Furnace, Anvil

*Lines 13–16. The making becomes a forge. The poem reaches its loudest point.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 4 of 6

**Heading:** The Forge

**Body:**

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=13-16]*

> What the hammer? what the chain,
> In what furnace was thy brain?
> What the anvil? what dread grasp,
> Dare its deadly terrors clasp!

Yesterday the heart began. Today the workshop. This is the noisiest stanza in the poem and the only one to end with an exclamation mark.

You will be saying this stanza out loud at some point today. Try it now. Hear the consonants strike.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Mythology

**Heading:** Los the Smith

**Body:**
Blake spent his late life building a private mythology nearly as elaborate as a religion. At its centre stood Los, a smith of the imagination who hammered fallen souls into shapes they could survive in. Los was Blake's figure for the artist himself, the one who works with fire and metal at the edge of the world.

"The Tyger" was written before Los had a name in Blake's writing, but you can already feel that figure pacing in the background of this stanza. Whoever made the tiger was a smith. The poem is putting into your head the sound of a forge, even before it tells you that's what it's doing.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Hammer, Chain, Furnace, Anvil

**Body:**
Four blacksmith's tools in three lines. Hammer, chain, furnace, anvil. The poem has stopped asking who and started naming the equipment. Each tool gets only a question's worth of attention before the next one strikes. The lines have shortened. The pauses have multiplied. The whole stanza moves like a sequence of blows.

There is no main verb in the first three lines. Just nouns thrown at you. "What the hammer? what the chain." That construction is grammatically broken on purpose. The speaker no longer has time for full sentences. The making is too loud to talk over.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** 1794

**Heading:** Revolution and Terror

**Body:**
*Songs of Experience* went on sale in 1794. By then the French Revolution that Blake had cheered five years earlier had become the Reign of Terror. The guillotine ran daily. Robespierre would fall in July. In England, Pitt's government was suspending habeas corpus and prosecuting radicals.

The forge in this stanza is not only the workshop of a god. It is also the world Blake was watching from his rented rooms in Lambeth: a furnace that promised to remake everything and was burning many of its own makers. He never said the poem was about the Revolution. He never said it was not.

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Where the Grammar Breaks

**Body:**
"Dare its deadly terrors clasp!" is the first line in the poem to end with an exclamation mark, and the only one. The grammar that has been holding throughout the poem finally breaks here. You can read the line as a question with the question mark dropped, or as a fragment, or as a kind of cry. None of those readings is wrong. The line is meant to overrun any one of them.

Notice the alliteration too: dare, deadly, dread back two lines, the d's hammering. The stanza arrives at noise, and after this stanza the poem has nowhere to go but quiet.

# DAY 5 — When the Stars Threw Down Their Spears

*Lines 17–20. The cosmos quiets, and the central question is finally spoken.*

---

## Page 1 — Introduction

**Eyebrow:** Day 5 of 6

**Heading:** The Lamb in the Room

**Body:**

*[DISPLAY: Chunk | lines=17-20]*

> When the stars threw down their spears
> And water'd heaven with their tears:
> Did he smile his work to see?
> Did he who made the Lamb make thee?

After the noise of the forge, this. Stars laying down their weapons. Heaven crying. And then the question Blake has been working towards from the first line.

If a single stanza of "The Tyger" makes it onto every page of every Blake anthology, it is this one. The closing line is one of the most quoted in English poetry.

---

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** The Companion Poem

**Heading:** Little Lamb who Made Thee?

**Body:**
Five years before "The Tyger", Blake published "The Lamb" in *Songs of Innocence*. It is a short, gentle poem in which a child speaks softly to a lamb in a field. "Little Lamb who made thee?" the child asks. And then the child answers: a meek and gentle maker, called by the same name, who became a little child himself. The poem is sweet without being saccharine, and Blake meant it.

When you read line twenty today you are reading a question that is being asked across five years and across two books. The Lamb is not a metaphor introduced for the first time here. The Lamb is a poem the reader is presumed to know.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Stars and Spears

**Body:**
"When the stars threw down their spears." Many readers hear an echo of the war in heaven, the moment in Christian and Miltonic tradition when the rebel angels surrender. Stars in Blake often stand for cold, distant powers. To throw down a spear is to give up the fight. The next line waters that surrender with weeping.

Whether the stars are weeping in defeat, in grief at what has been made, or in something simpler that we cannot quite name, the poem refuses to settle. What it gives you instead is a hush. After four stanzas of hammer and fire, the universe goes very still and looks at what it has produced.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Belief

**Heading:** Blake and the Sacred

**Body:**
Blake was a deeply religious man and a furious enemy of the church. He believed in Christ but considered most clergy to be hypocrites and most churches to be prisons. He wrote his own scripture, drew his own visions, and wandered around London arguing with archangels in his garden. None of this was performance. He meant every word.

When he asks "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?" it is not a rhetorical flourish. He is asking the oldest question religion has, the one theologians call the problem of evil, in its barest possible form. He does not pretend to have an answer.

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** The Question Spoken Plain

**Body:**
After more than a dozen questions in winding, abstract language, the poem finally asks the question it has been hiding. "Did he who made the Lamb make thee?" There is no Latinate vocabulary. No Promethean reaching. Just a one-syllable verb, a familiar pronoun, and two of the smallest nouns in English.

Look at line nineteen too: "Did he smile his work to see?" The image is almost intolerable. A creator standing back from the finished tiger and being pleased with it. Blake does not flinch. He places the smile in the line and waits for you to decide what to do with it.

## Page 2 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Afterwards

**Heading:** How Blake Was Lost and Found

**Body:**
Blake died in 1827, aged sixty-nine, sitting up in bed singing hymns of his own composition. His wife held his hand. He was buried in a common grave in Bunhill Fields. For decades almost no one read him. The literary establishment of his lifetime had treated him as eccentric or mad, and after his death it lost interest entirely.

He was rescued in stages. The Pre-Raphaelite poets and painters in the 1860s recovered his books. The twentieth century turned him into a giant. The poem you are finishing today was, in his lifetime, sold from his front room in Lambeth in handfuls of copies. It is now one of the most-quoted poems in the language. Blake never knew.

## Page 4 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** Could Becomes Dare

**Body:**
The first stanza ends with "Could frame thy fearful symmetry?" The last stanza ends with "Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?" Everything else in the four lines is the same.

That single substitution does the whole work of the poem. "Could" was a question of capacity: who is even able. "Dare" is a question of will: who is willing to take responsibility for having done this. Across twenty lines we have moved from wondering whether the maker exists to wondering whether the maker can stand to look at what was made. The poem ends, in other words, by changing the question rather than answering it.

## Page 6 — Background

**Eyebrow:** Legacy

**Heading:** Where the Poem Travelled

**Body:**
"The Tyger" went on to become probably the most-anthologised short poem in English. It is the epigraph to dozens of novels. It has been set to music more times than anyone has counted. Children meet it before they meet most other poetry, often without realising what it is asking them.

There is a small irony in that, and Blake might have appreciated it. The man who could not give his book away in 1794 wrote four lines that several billion people now know by heart. Though, to be fair to him, he probably suspected this would happen all along.

> What is now proved was once, only imagin'd.
> — William Blake

## Page 8 — Analysis

**Eyebrow:** Close Reading

**Heading:** The Unanswered Ending

**Body:**
Count the questions in the poem. There are roughly thirteen, depending on how you parse the fragments. Blake answers none of them. The last stanza does not even pretend to be an answer; it is the first stanza asked a second time, harder.

That is the point. "The Tyger" is not a poem about a tiger. It is a poem about what it feels like to stand in a world that contains both the Lamb and the Tyger, and to be unable to reconcile the two. The poem refuses to do the reconciling for you. It hands you the questions and walks away.
